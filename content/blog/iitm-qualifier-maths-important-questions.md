## What the Maths I qualifier tests

Maths I is "Mathematics for Data Science I" (course code BSMA1001). The qualifier covers **weeks 1 to 4** only. The official [Maths I course page on study.iitm.ac.in](https://study.iitm.ac.in/ds/course_pages/BSMA1001.html) lists the full syllabus. For the qualifier, these are the four weeks:

| Week | Topics |
|---|---|
| 1 | Sets, relations and functions |
| 2 | Coordinate geometry and straight lines |
| 3 | Quadratic functions |
| 4 | Polynomials |

Limits, derivatives, integration and graph theory are also in Maths I. But they come **after week 4**. In our tagging, not one qualifier question came from those topics. They show up only in [Maths I Quiz 1](/pyqs/foundation/mathematics-for-data-science-1/quiz-1-oct-2025-afternoon), Quiz 2 and the [Maths I end term exam](/pyqs/foundation/mathematics-for-data-science-1/end-term-dec-2024-forenoon). So do not spend qualifier time on them.

To pass, a General-category candidate needs at least 40% in Maths I and at least 50% average across all four courses. Check the current rules on the official [IITM BS admissions page](https://study.iitm.ac.in/ds/admissions.html).

What this tells you:

- **Weeks 3 and 4 are worth the most.** Quadratics plus polynomials give 107 of 240 marks (about 45%). Many students spend too long on sets in week 1 and rush the last two weeks. Do the opposite.

## Solved IITM qualifier maths important questions

These are four real questions from past qualifier papers. Each final answer matches the official answer key in the paper data on BTechi.

### Solved example 1: three-set Venn counting (May 2024, Q6, 4 marks)

**Question.** 500 candidates applied for a data analyst post. 285 know Python, 195 know C, 115 know Java. 45 know Python and Java, 70 know C and Python, 50 know C and Java. 50 candidates know none of the three. How many know **exactly one** language?

**Solution.**

1. At least one language = 500 − 50 = **450**.
2. Use inclusion-exclusion: 450 = (285 + 195 + 115) − (45 + 70 + 50) + x, where x = all three.
3. 450 = 595 − 165 + x = 430 + x, so **x = 20**.
4. Exactly two: Python and C only = 70 − 20 = 50. Python and Java only = 45 − 20 = 25. C and Java only = 50 − 20 = 30.
5. Exactly one: Python only = 285 − 50 − 25 − 20 = 190. C only = 195 − 50 − 30 − 20 = 95. Java only = 115 − 25 − 30 − 20 = 40.
6. Answer: 190 + 95 + 40 = **325**. The official key says 325.

![Three-set Venn diagram for the May 2024 qualifier question, filled from the centre: 20 in all three, 50, 25 and 30 in exactly two, and 190, 95 and 40 in exactly one.](/blog/iitm-qualifier-maths-important-questions/venn-centre-out.svg "Start with 'all three', then 'exactly two', then 'exactly one'. Never start from the outside.")

**Why students lose marks here:** they add 285 + 195 + 115 and subtract the pair counts, but forget that each pair count already includes the "all three" group. Always fill the centre first.

Try more on the [sets and Venn diagram topic sheet](/pyqs/foundation/mathematics-for-data-science-1/sheets/topic-sets-and-venn-diagrams). The [January 2024 paper](/pyqs/foundation/mathematics-for-data-science-1/qualifier-january-2024) has a two-part version (60 grocery customers; answers 11 and 2).

### Solved example 2: relations (May 2024, Q1 to Q3)

**Question.** A is the set of even positive integers up to 20. B is the set of positive integers less than 20 that are divisible by 6. Two relations from A to B:

- R₁ = {(a, b) : a is a factor of b}
- R₂ = {(a, b) : (a + b) mod 10 = 0}

Find the cardinality of R₁ ∩ R₂ (3 marks), the cardinality of R₁ (2 marks), and which statements are true (3 marks): R₁ is transitive; R₂ is transitive; R₂ is not symmetric; (2, 18) is in R₂.

**Solution.**

1. Write the sets first. A = {2, 4, 6, ..., 20}. B = {6, 12, 18}.
2. **List R₁.** For b = 6: a = 2, 6. For b = 12: a = 2, 4, 6, 12. For b = 18: a = 2, 6, 18. So **|R₁| = 9**.
3. **List R₂.** For b = 6: a = 4, 14. For b = 12: a = 8, 18. For b = 18: a = 2, 12. So R₂ has 6 pairs.
4. **R₁ ∩ R₂.** The only pair in both lists is (2, 18). So **|R₁ ∩ R₂| = 1**.
5. **Statements.** R₁ is transitive, because "is a factor of" is transitive. R₂ is not transitive: (2, 18) and (18, 12) are in R₂, but (2, 12) is not, since 2 + 12 = 14. R₂ is not symmetric: (2, 18) is in R₂ but (18, 2) is not, since 2 is not in B. And (2, 18) is in R₂ because 20 mod 10 = 0.
6. Correct statements: **R₁ is transitive, R₂ is not symmetric, (2, 18) is in R₂**.

Official key: 1, 9, and options 1, 3 and 4. All match.

**The habit that wins:** write every relation as a list of pairs. Then test each property against the list. Reasoning from the rule alone is where mistakes happen. Practise this on the [relations and functions topic sheet](/pyqs/foundation/mathematics-for-data-science-1/sheets/topic-relations-and-functions).

### Solved example 3: straight lines (January 2024, Q6 and Q7, 3 marks each)

**Question.** Lines L₁ and L₂ have x-intercepts 2 and −1, and y-intercepts −3 and 2. Find where they meet, and find tan θ, where θ is the angle between them.

**Solution.**

1. Intercepts are points. L₁ passes through (2, 0) and (0, −3). Slope m₁ = (−3 − 0) / (0 − 2) = **3/2**. Equation: y = 1.5x − 3.
2. L₂ passes through (−1, 0) and (0, 2). Slope m₂ = (2 − 0) / (0 + 1) = **2**. Equation: y = 2x + 2.
3. Intersection: 1.5x − 3 = 2x + 2, so −0.5x = 5 and **x = −10**. Then y = 2(−10) + 2 = **−18**. The point is (−10, −18).
4. tan θ = |(m₂ − m₁) / (1 + m₁m₂)| = |(2 − 1.5) / (1 + 3)| = 0.5 / 4 = **1/8**.

Official key: (−10, −18) and 1/8. Both match.

The May 2024 paper asked the same two types with different numbers (its tan θ answer is 5/7). The [straight lines topic sheet](/pyqs/foundation/mathematics-for-data-science-1/sheets/topic-coordinate-geometry-lines) has 16 questions like this.

### Solved example 4: polynomial word problem (January 2023, Q8, 5 marks)

**Question.** A company starts in 2010. Its yearly profit (in lakhs) is p(x) = 0.1x²(x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 3)²(x − 4)(x − 9), where x is years since 2010. Loss means negative profit. Which are correct?

- (a) Including the launch year, it made neither profit nor loss six times in 12 years.
- (b) It made profit in years x ∈ {7, 8, 10, 11}.
- (c) It made loss in years x ∈ {5, 6, 7, 8}.
- (d) In 2022 (x = 12) it made profit.

**Solution.**

1. **Zeros.** p(x) = 0 at x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 9. That is six values, all within the 12 years. So **(a) is true**.
2. **Sign.** x² and (x − 3)² are never negative. So the sign of p(x) depends only on (x − 1)(x − 2)(x − 4)(x − 9).
3. For x = 5, 6, 7, 8: the first three brackets are positive and (x − 9) is negative. So p(x) < 0, a loss. **(c) is true**, and **(b) is false**, because 7 and 8 are loss years.
4. For x = 12: every bracket is positive. Profit. **(d) is true**.

Official key: (a), (c) and (d). It matches.

**The shortcut:** squared factors never change the sign. Ignore them when you check profit or loss. This "sign of each bracket" method also solves most graph-reading questions on the [polynomials topic sheet](/pyqs/foundation/mathematics-for-data-science-1/sheets/topic-polynomials).

## Formulas to know cold for Maths I

| You need | Formula |
|---|---|
| Three-set union | \|A ∪ B ∪ C\| = Σ\|A\| − Σ\|A ∩ B\| + \|A ∩ B ∩ C\| |
| Exactly one of three sets | Σ\|A\| − 2Σ\|A ∩ B\| + 3\|A ∩ B ∩ C\| |
| Slope from two points | (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁) |
| Angle between two lines | tan θ = \|(m₁ − m₂) / (1 + m₁m₂)\| |
| Perpendicular lines | m₁ × m₂ = −1 |
| Vertex of ax² + bx + c | x = −b/2a |
| Sum and product of roots | −b/a and c/a |
| Rational roots of a quadratic (integer coefficients) | Discriminant b² − 4ac is a perfect square |
| SSE for a line | Σ(actual y − predicted y)² |

## Mistakes that cost marks in Maths I

Common ways students lose marks in the Maths I papers:

- **MSQ partial thinking.** In multiple-select questions, judge each option alone, as true or false. Do not stop after you find one true option.
- **Relations across two sets.** When R is from A to B, symmetry needs (b, a) to be a valid pair too. If b is not in A, the pair cannot exist.
- **Intercept confusion.** "x-intercept 2" means the point (2, 0). "y-intercept −3" means (0, −3). Write the point down first.
- **Even multiplicity.** A root with an even power touches the x-axis and turns back. It does not cross. Graph questions test this almost every time.
- **Numerical answer format.** Some NAT questions ask you to type option numbers joined together (May 2023 Q1 and Q2 did this). Read the instruction twice.

### What are the most important topics for the IITM qualifier maths exam?

Polynomials (25.4% of marks), quadratic functions (19.2%) and straight lines (17.1%), based on our tagging of five past Maths I qualifier papers. Relations, three-set Venn diagrams and best-fit lines (SSE) come next. All six appear in almost every paper.

### Are IITM qualifier maths questions repeated?

Yes. Question **types** repeat often: three-set counting, polynomial graphs and relation properties each appeared in four of five papers. One full question (the 500-candidates Python, C and Java problem) appeared in both May 2023 and May 2024 with the same numbers.

### Do I need calculus for the IITM qualifier?

No. The qualifier covers weeks 1 to 4 only: sets, relations, functions, straight lines, quadratics and polynomials. In five past qualifier papers, no question came from limits, derivatives or integrals. Those topics come later in Maths I.

### How many questions are in the Maths I qualifier paper?

The five papers on BTechi have 10 to 14 Maths I questions each, worth 40 to 50 marks. Most are numerical answer or multiple-select questions. The exact count and marking can change, so read the instructions on exam day.

### Is the answer key for the Maths I qualifier PYQ official?

Yes. The answers on BTechi are from the official answer keys of the IIT Madras BS papers. Where a numerical answer accepts a range, the range is shown.

### How many days should I spend on Maths I important questions?

There is no fixed number. A practical approach: solve one topic sheet after each week's lectures, then the most repeated sheet and two full papers in the last week. Spend more time on polynomials and quadratics, since they carry the most marks.
