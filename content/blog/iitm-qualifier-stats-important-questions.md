## Solved IITM qualifier stats important questions

Four real questions from past Stats I qualifier papers. Each final answer matches the official answer key in the paper data on BTechi.

### Solved example 1: scales of measurement (May 2024, Q7, 3 marks)

**Question.** Consider three statements:

- Statement 1: Election symbol is a categorical variable.
- Statement 2: Election symbol has a nominal scale of measurement.
- Statement 3: Number of votes received by a candidate is a continuous variable.

Which are correct? (a) 2 and 3, (b) 1 and 3, (c) 1 and 2, (d) all three.

**Solution.**

1. An election symbol is a label. You cannot add it or average it. So it is **categorical**. Statement 1 is true.
2. Symbols have no natural order. A lotus is not "more" than a hand. Labels with no order are **nominal**. Statement 2 is true.
3. Votes are counted: 0, 1, 2, and so on. You cannot get 1,250.5 votes. Counts are **discrete**, not continuous. Statement 3 is false.
4. Answer: **(c) Statement 1 and Statement 2**. The official key says (c).

**The rule to remember:** count = discrete, measure = continuous. Labels with no order = nominal. Labels with an order (like shirt size S, M, L) = ordinal. Practise on the [data types and scales topic sheet](/pyqs/foundation/statistics-for-data-science-1/sheets/topic-data-types-and-scales).

### Solved example 2: mode and median of ordinal data (January 2024, Q7 and Q8)

**Question.** A survey of 10 students rated the quality of education as: Fair, Fair, Excellent, Poor, Fair, Excellent, Poor, Poor, Excellent, Poor. Find the mode (1 mark) and the median (2 marks).

**Solution.**

1. Count each category. Poor = 4, Fair = 3, Excellent = 3.
2. **Mode** is the most frequent category: **Poor**.
3. For the median, the data must have an order. Poor < Fair < Excellent. So this is ordinal data, and the median is defined.
4. Sort: Poor, Poor, Poor, Poor, Fair, Fair, Fair, Excellent, Excellent, Excellent.
5. With 10 values, the median sits between the 5th and 6th values. Both are Fair. So the **median is Fair**.

Official key: mode Poor, median Fair. Both match.

**The trap:** for nominal data (like soft drink brands), the median is **not defined**, because there is no order. The May 2024 paper (Q1) tested exactly this: the correct options were "the data is bimodal" and "median is not defined". More of these are on the [categorical data topic sheet](/pyqs/foundation/statistics-for-data-science-1/sheets/topic-describing-categorical-data).

### Solved example 3: contingency table (May 2024, Q5 and Q6)

**Question.** A survey of 32 college students (male and female) asks if they own a smartphone. 3 males do not own one. There are 27 females. In total, 26 students do not own a smartphone. (a) How many males own a smartphone? (b) Which statements are true?

- 40% of males do not own a smartphone.
- 14.81% of females own a smartphone.
- 18.75% of all students own a smartphone.
- We can use covariance to find the association between gender and smartphone ownership.

**Solution.** Build the two-way table first. Fill the given numbers, then use the totals.

| | Owns | Does not own | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Male | **2** | 3 | 5 |
| Female | 4 | 23 | 27 |
| Total | 6 | 26 | 32 |

1. Males = 32 − 27 = 5. Males who own = 5 − 3 = **2**. That answers (a).
2. Females who do not own = 26 − 3 = 23. Females who own = 27 − 23 = 4. Total who own = 32 − 26 = 6.
3. Males who do not own: 3/5 = 60%, not 40%. **False**.
4. Females who own: 4/27 = 14.81%. **True**.
5. All students who own: 6/32 = 18.75%. **True**.
6. Gender and ownership are categorical. Covariance needs numbers. **False**. Use relative frequencies to judge association instead.

Official key: (a) 2; (b) the second and third statements. Both match.

**The habit that wins:** always draw the full table with totals before you read any option. Most wrong answers come from dividing by the wrong total (row total vs column total vs grand total). The [association topic sheet](/pyqs/foundation/statistics-for-data-science-1/sheets/topic-association-two-variables) has 20 questions to drill this.

### Solved example 4: quartiles and outliers (May 2023, Q13 to Q15, 3 marks each)

**Question.** Prices (in rupees) of 10 t-shirts: 150, 550, 700, 240, 300, 750, 200, 180, 320, 420. Find the price below which 25% of t-shirts fall, the price that separates the top 25%, and the number of outliers.

**Solution.** Use the IITM percentile method, which the official answer keys follow:

1. Sort: 150, 180, 200, 240, 300, 320, 420, 550, 700, 750. Here n = 10.
2. For the P-th percentile, find i = P × n / 100. If i is not a whole number, round **up** and take that value. If i is whole, average the values at positions i and i + 1.
3. **Q1 (25th percentile):** i = 0.25 × 10 = 2.5. Round up to 3. The 3rd value is **200**.
4. **Q3 (75th percentile):** i = 0.75 × 10 = 7.5. Round up to 8. The 8th value is **550**.
5. IQR = 550 − 200 = 350. 1.5 × IQR = 525.
6. Fences: lower = 200 − 525 = −325, upper = 550 + 525 = 1075. Every price is between them. **Number of outliers = 0**.

Official key: Q1 = Rs 200, Q3 = Rs 550, outliers = 0. All match.

![The 10 sorted t-shirt prices with Q1 = 200 at position 3 and Q3 = 550 at position 8, and a number line showing all prices inside the fences of −325 and 1075.](/blog/iitm-qualifier-stats-important-questions/quartiles-outliers.svg "Sort first, find the position, round up. Then check every value against the two fences.")

The same method gives the September 2023 answer too: for 14, 20, 40, 23, 18, sorted as 14, 18, 20, 23, 40, Q1 is the 2nd value (18) and Q3 is the 4th (23), so IQR = 5. That matches the official key. Drill this on the [dispersion and percentiles topic sheet](/pyqs/foundation/statistics-for-data-science-1/sheets/topic-dispersion-and-percentiles).

## Rules to know cold for Stats I

| You need | Rule |
|---|---|
| Add a constant c to every value | Mean, median, mode, quartiles go up by c. Variance, SD and IQR do not change |
| Multiply every value by a | Mean, median, quartiles multiply by a. SD and IQR multiply by \|a\|. Variance multiplies by a² |
| Correlation of X with aX + b (a > 0) | Exactly 1 |
| Percentile position | i = P × n / 100. Fraction: round up. Whole: average positions i and i + 1 |
| Outlier fences | Q1 − 1.5 × IQR and Q3 + 1.5 × IQR |
| Fix a wrong value in the mean | New sum = old sum − wrong value + right value |
| Fix a wrong value in the variance | Σx² = (n − 1)s² + n × mean², then swap the value in both sums |
| No association in a contingency table | Row relative frequencies are about the same in every row |
| Sample vs population | Conclusions about the whole group from a sample = inferential statistics |

The linear-change rules alone cover three repeated types: mean after a change, variance after a change, and correlation with a linear function. Learn them once and you collect marks in almost every paper.

## Mistakes that cost marks in Stats I

- **Median of nominal data.** It is not defined. Only ordinal and numerical data have a median.
- **Population vs sample variance.** Divide by n for population, n − 1 for sample. The question always says which one. Read it.
- **Wrong total in a contingency table.** "Of all males" means divide by the male total, not the grand total.
- **School percentile methods.** Use the IITM method (round up the position). Other methods can give a different answer and lose the marks.
- **Representative samples.** A sample of only final-year students does not represent all students (September 2023, Q3). Check who is in the sample, not just its size.

### How many questions are in the Stats I qualifier paper?

The five papers on BTechi have 13 to 18 Stats I questions each, worth 40 marks. They mix numerical answer, multiple-select and single-correct questions. The exact count and marking can change, so read the exam instructions on the day.

### Which percentile method does IITM use in Stats I?

Find the position i = P × n / 100 in the sorted data. If i is a fraction, round up and take that value. If i is a whole number, average the values at positions i and i + 1. The official answer keys follow this method.

### Is Stats I easier than Maths I in the qualifier?

Many students find Stats I more scoring, because many questions are short theory and chart questions. But it has more questions and more traps in percentiles and contingency tables. Do not take it lightly. You still need 40% in it to qualify.
